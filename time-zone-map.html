How time zones work

The Earth rotates 360° every 24 hours, so local solar time shifts by roughly one hour for every 15° of longitude. To keep clocks practical, the world is divided into time zones — regions that share a single standard time expressed as an offset from UTC. Most offsets are whole hours, but a few regions use 30- or 45-minute offsets, and many observe daylight saving time for part of the year.

Frequently asked questions

What is a time zone map?

A time zone map shows how the world is divided into regions that share the same standard time, expressed as an offset from UTC. It lets you see the current local time anywhere at a glance.

How many time zones are there?

There are 24 main hourly zones from UTC−12 to UTC+14, but including half-hour and 45-minute offsets (like India at UTC+5:30 or Nepal at UTC+5:45) there are around 38 distinct offsets in use.

What is UTC?

UTC (Coordinated Universal Time) is the global time standard all zones are defined against — for example UTC−5 (New York) or UTC+9 (Tokyo).

Does the map account for daylight saving time?

Yes. The live times use each location's current IANA time zone, which reflects daylight saving automatically, so the times shown are always the real local time.
